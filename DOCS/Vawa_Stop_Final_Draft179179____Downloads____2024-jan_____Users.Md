## PART THREE: THE FUNDING ARCHITECTURE

California received VAWA STOP formula grant funds under CFDA 16.588 in each fiscal year from 2021 through 2024. The confirmed FY2023 award was $16,104,812 in STOP funds and $2,648,245 under the Sexual Assault Services Program; the four-year cumulative award to California is estimated at approximately $49 million with only FY2023 confirmed from primary source documentation. Federal statute distributes STOP funds according to fixed set-aside floors: 25 percent to law enforcement, 25 percent to prosecution, 5 percent to courts, 30 percent to victim services, with state administration capped at 10 percent. These are statutory minimums — the floor below which distribution cannot lawfully fall. The floor matters because it is the floor that was not reached, and because the gap between the floor and the confirmed zero-dollar allocations at the county's largest law enforcement agency, its superior court, and its direct survivor assistance programs is not an audit finding in the abstract. It is the operative condition under which every institutional contact this document describes occurred.

DOJ OIG Audit 25-038, released January 2025, identified the structural reason the floor was not reached in practice. Cal OES consolidates multiple federal funding streams — STOP, VOCA, FVPSA, and state funds — into single combined subawards. Subrecipients receive consolidated awards and cannot separately account for each federal source. The traceability of VAWA STOP funds is eliminated at the state level before the money reaches any county subrecipient, before any compliance obligation can be verified against it, and before any audit can confirm that the statutory set-aside floors were honored. The mandate descends from federal statute. The accountability mechanism that would confirm it was honored dissolves below the state level. That is a structural defect — identified by the OIG, documented in the audit record, and demonstrated in practice by Santa Barbara County's confirmed allocation figures.

**Powell v. Alabama**

In 1931, nine Black teenagers in Alabama were charged with rape, arraigned, and tried within days. The trial judge appointed counsel by designating all members of the bar collectively — a gesture the Supreme Court subsequently determined imposed no actual obligation on any identified individual. No lawyer prepared a defense. No lawyer investigated the facts or interviewed witnesses. Trials were completed within a single day. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), reversed those convictions on a single constitutional ground: the defendants had been denied the right to counsel in any substantive sense.

The Court named the gap precisely: it is vain to give the accused a day in court with no opportunity to prepare for it, or to guarantee him counsel without giving the latter any opportunity to acquaint himself with the facts or law of the case. It named the population most exposed to that gap: he requires the guiding hand of counsel at every step in the proceedings against him; without it, though he be not guilty, he faces the danger of conviction because he does not know how to establish his innocence.
