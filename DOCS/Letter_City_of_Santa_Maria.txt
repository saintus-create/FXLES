LETTER TO THE CITY OF SANTA MARIA

The Payment Structure, and the Shell Through Which Its Domestic-Violence
Money Moves

[Date]

To the Mayor and City Council of the City of Santa Maria; the City Manager;
and the Chief of Police.

Re:  The City's payment structure, and the shell through which its
     domestic-violence money moves


Santa Maria is the largest city in Santa Barbara County. It has been since
2006. Its 112,000 residents are a quarter of the county's population. The
department that serves them answered 247,000 calls for service in fiscal
year 2022-23. Weapons-involved domestic-violence calls in the county rose
148 percent between 2013 and 2022. In 2017 the District Attorney stated
publicly that domestic violence is the most predictable homicide in the
county.

This letter sets out two things: what the City pays, and where the money
that was supposed to reach its officers went instead.


I.  WHAT THE CITY PAYS

The City's audited financial statements for fiscal year 2023 record $20.59
million in claims payable and $6.87 million in vacancy savings. Among the
claims is a settlement of $1,028,612.41 for an illegal SWAT raid executed
on a false warrant. As of June 30, 2023 the department carried nearly $21
million in confirmed civil liability claims.

That is the city's payment structure on the liability side. What follows is
the payment structure on the funding side, which is the one that decides
what its officers know when they arrive at a door.


II.  WHAT THE CITY RECEIVES, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT

California received $44,327,900 in confirmed Violence Against Women Act
STOP awards between fiscal years 2021 and 2023. Federal law requires 25
percent of that - approximately $11 million - to reach law enforcement
agencies.

The Santa Maria Police Department received none of it. Not in fiscal 2021.
Not in fiscal 2022. Not in fiscal 2023. The county's independently audited
Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards shows no VAWA Formula Grant
entry from fiscal 2009-10 through fiscal 2023-24. The only confirmed VAWA
STOP law enforcement award to any Santa Barbara County agency is a single
$203,143 grant to the Sheriff in fiscal 2024 - the same year as the first
arrest.

The department had the capacity to pursue grants. During the same period
its Traffic Bureau secured grant funding for collision enforcement and
deployed four dedicated motorcycle officers to school zones. The state
simultaneously sent the department non-competitive Officer Wellness funds
under Assembly Bill 178 - a check based on officer headcount, no
application required, no nondiscrimination conditions attached. Officers
were funded to manage their own mental health. The money meant to help them
recognize a victim's did not arrive.


III.  THE SHELL

Federal STOP grants flow from the Department of Justice to the California
Office of Emergency Services. Cal OES consolidates STOP funds with VOCA,
FVPSA, and state funds into single combined subawards, so that
subrecipients cannot separately account for each federal source. From Cal
OES the training money flows to the San Diego Regional Training Center as
the statewide fiscal agent, which subcontracts portal operations to
Performa Labs Incorporated.

Performa Labs is a Delaware corporation founded in 2019, headquartered in
Irvine, California, with between two and ten employees and $7.64 million in
total funding raised. The Los Angeles Police Department's City Attorney
determined in August 2023 that no competing product exists and competitive
bidding was impossible. Performa Labs is the sole-source vendor for the only
training mechanism California law enforcement is required to use for
domestic-violence response. The subcontract amount is not publicly
disclosed.

The corporate paper behind the vendor runs through a special-purpose
acquisition company. The SEC filing for Riverview Acquisition Corp. (CIK
1846136, July 22, 2021) records a directorship held since 2019 by the same
individual connected to Performa Labs. A special-purpose acquisition company
is a shell by construction: a listed entity with no operations, formed to
acquire something else. The training the state mandates for its officers is
delivered through a vendor whose corporate ancestry includes one - and the
money for it passes through a five-intermediary chain, each link of which
takes its share before anything reaches a police department.


IV.  THE OFFICERS

California Penal Code section 13519(g) requires in-service patrol officers
to complete two hours of domestic-violence training every two years. That
training is delivered through the POST Learning Portal.

Two hours. Every two years. Not scenario-tested. No agency verifies whether
it changed anything at the point of contact. The California POST Course
Reimbursement Statistics Report for fiscal year 2022 shows the Santa Maria
Police Department absent as a training reimbursement claimant for any course
in any category.


V.  THE OUTCOME

In October 2024, two officers from the Santa Maria Police Department
responded to a domestic-violence call and arrested the victim instead of the
abuser. The man who placed the call had filed a restraining-order petition
in March 2024 containing no specific dates, no specific locations, no
conduct satisfying the statutory definition of domestic violence, and the
wrong pronoun for the person it restrained. The court accepted it the same
day.

The funding did not fail to arrive because anyone decided to harm the person
who was arrested. It failed to arrive because the architecture that was
supposed to deliver it was never built to reach the largest city in the
county, was never updated when that city outgrew the design, and was never
audited for whether the statutory floor it was supposed to meet was actually
met at the agency level where it was supposed to matter.

The city pays for the consequences - in claims, in settlements, in a SWAT
raid executed on a false warrant - while the money designated to prevent
them moves through a shell and stops before it reaches the officers.


VI.  WHAT THE CITY CAN ASK

1.  An itemized allocation, by agency and by fiscal year, of every VAWA
    STOP dollar that reached Santa Barbara County, and confirmation whether
    the statutory 25 percent law enforcement floor was met.

2.  The amount of the San Diego Regional Training Center subcontract with
    Performa Labs, and the terms on which the sole-source determination was
    made.

3.  Whether the department has ever applied for a VAWA STOP law
    enforcement subaward, and if not, why the Traffic Bureau's grant
    capacity was not directed to it.

4.  An accounting of the $3.1 million in uncommitted POST training funds,
    and whether any part of it can be directed to departments that receive
    no training subaward at all.
