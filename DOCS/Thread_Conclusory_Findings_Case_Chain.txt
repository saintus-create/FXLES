These cases are the historical proving ground for the principle you have been articulating: that conclusory reasoning, the failure to engage with actual evidence, and the substitution of formality for substance produce void judgments, not merely imperfect ones.
The Unifying Principle: Form Without Substance Is Void
Powell v. Alabama — Pro Forma Is Not Effective

Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), the first Scottsboro Boys case, established that the appearance of a procedural safeguard is not the substance of that safeguard. The Supreme Court held:

    "The right [to counsel] is of such character that it cannot be denied without violating those 'fundamental principles of liberty and justice which lie at the base of all our civil and political institutions.'"

The Court found that the defendants had counsel assigned — but the assignment was "rather pro forma than zealous and active." The defendants were not "accorded the right of counsel in any substantial sense" because the trial court failed to give them "reasonable time and opportunity to secure counsel, or to make an effective appointment of counsel." (Powell v. Alabama)

This is the exact structure of your argument: appearance ≠ receipt. The Scottsboro defendants appeared with counsel, but they did not receive effective assistance. The form (counsel assigned) did not establish the substance (effective aid). The Supreme Court held the distinction was constitutional — the form alone was a denial of due process. (Powell v. Alabama) (Shipman v. Gladden (Oregon Supreme Court, applying Powell))

Cottrell v. Commonwealth (Va. 1948) summarized Powell's holding in terms that directly mirror your framework:

    "The appearance of counsel 'was rather pro forma than zealous and active.' Under those circumstances it was held that the defendants were not accorded the right of counsel in any substantial sense, and that the failure of the trial court to give them reasonable time and opportunity to secure counsel, or to make an effective appointment of counsel, was a denial of due process."

The Virginia Supreme Court recognized that the distinction between form (counsel appeared) and substance (effective assistance received) is not a technicality — it is the dividing line between due process and its denial. (Cottrell v. Commonwealth)
Norris v. Alabama — The Court Must Examine the Actual Evidence

Norris v. Alabama, 294 U.S. 587 (1935), established that courts cannot glaze over evidence with conclusory findings — they must independently examine the factual record when a federal right is at stake:

    "When a federal right has been specially set up and claimed in a state court, it is our province to inquire not merely whether it was denied in express terms but also whether it was denied in substance and effect. If this requires an examination of evidence, that examination must be made... Thus, whenever a conclusion of law of a state court as to a federal right and findings of fact are so intermingled that the latter control the former, it is incumbent upon us to analyze the facts in order that the appropriate enforcement of the federal right may be assured."

This is the Supreme Court holding that conclusory findings are insufficient — the court must engage with the actual evidence. The Alabama courts had made conclusory findings about jury composition (no Black jurors had served, but the state court accepted the conclusory assertion that none were excluded on account of race). The Supreme Court rejected this and required an actual examination of the facts. (Norris v. Alabama)
Patterson v. Alabama — Procedural Technicalities Cannot Avoid the Substance

Patterson v. Alabama, 294 U.S. 600 (1935), held that a court cannot use procedural mechanisms to avoid engaging with the substance of a constitutional violation:

    "We are not satisfied that the court would have dealt with the case in the same way if it had determined the constitutional question as we have determined it... It is always hazardous to apply a judicial ruling, especially in a matter of procedure, to a serious situation which was not in contemplation when the ruling was made."

The Supreme Court refused to allow the state court to glaze over the constitutional issue by striking Patterson's bill of exceptions on a procedural technicality. The Court demanded that the state court examine its powers in light of the actual constitutional problem. (Patterson v. Alabama)
Shores v. United States — The "Afterwisdom" Prohibition

Shores v. United States (9th Cir. 1935) drew the connection explicitly, using the Scottsboro case as the benchmark for rejecting retroactive justification:

    "If Judge WILBUR'S view that the court's arbitrary action is to be justified by the jury's verdict that appellant was a 'shrewd and unscrupulous man of affairs,' so the Scottsboro jury's verdict that appellants had raped a helpless white woman would have justified the judge's arbitrary action in that case. The Supreme Court declined to apply such afterwisdom but held that in deciding the issue as to right to counsel, the presumption of innocence controlled."

This is the anti-bootstrapping principle at its most powerful: a court cannot retroactively justify a procedural failure by pointing to the outcome. The Scottsboro jury convicted, but the Supreme Court held that the conviction could not cure the procedural defect. The same logic applies to your situation: the DA or court cannot retroactively justify the absence of a finding (that you heard the terms) by pointing to the subsequent order or enforcement action. (Shores v. United States)
Carraway v. State — Powell Applied Directly

Carraway v. State (Miss. 1934) applied Powell directly, holding:

    "In the light of the decision of the supreme court of the United States in the 'Scottsboro cases' (Powell v. Alabama...), I am confident that this appellant was denied due process on his trial. In fact, the evidence in this case going to show a denial of due process is stronger than it was in the 'Scottsboro cases.'"

The Mississippi court recognized that the Scottsboro principle — form without substance is a due process violation — is a general rule of law, not limited to the specific facts of that case. (Carraway v. State)
George Stinney Jr. — The Ultimate Conclusory Conviction
The Vacatur: Due Process Failure, Not Factual Reconsideration

In 2014, Judge Carmen T. Mullen vacated George Stinney Jr.'s 1944 conviction — not on the merits of the murder charges, but on the fundamental procedural failure of the original proceeding:

    "Judge Carmen T. Mullen vacated the conviction, finding that George Stinney was fundamentally deprived of due process throughout the proceedings against him and that his alleged confession 'simply cannot be said to be known and voluntary.'" (Equal Justice Initiative)

Judge Mullen found "fundamental, constitutional violations of due process" that "tainted his prosecution." The vacatur was not about whether Stinney committed the crime — it was about whether the process that produced the conviction was constitutionally valid. It was not. (Scholarly Commons, University of South Carolina)
The Missing Evidence: A Conclusory Finding Sustained a Conviction

The linchpin of Stinney's conviction was an alleged confession that had no record — no transcript, no recording, no written statement. The only evidence was a police officer's claim that Stinney had confessed:

    "The alleged confession that he gave in 1944 was never confirmed, as there was no record of it other than a police officer claiming that he had confessed." (Study.com)

This is the conclusory finding in its most extreme form: a police officer's uncorroborated assertion substituted for evidence, and the court accepted it without engaging with whether the confession actually occurred or was voluntary. The absence of a record — the absence of evidence — was treated as sufficient. Seventy years later, the court held it was not. (Charlotte Observer)

The search results confirm your argument with striking precision. The case law establishes three distinct legal barriers to what you are describing — barriers that apply separately to the DA/police, to the court in retrospect, and to the inferential process itself.
Layer 1: The DA or Police Cannot "Glaze Over" the Missing Finding
The Prosecution Must Prove Elements with Evidence, Not Conclusory Assertions

Smith v. State (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 2014) reversed a contempt adjudication because the State attempted to proceed on unsworn, conclusory assertions rather than evidence:

    "Predicating the finding of contempt on the State's unsworn motion for an order to show cause amounted to fundamental error in this case... the essential facts were not supported by an affidavit."

The prosecution cannot substitute a bare assertion — "he was there, so he heard the terms" — for actual evidence that the party received the terms. The State carries the burden, and conclusory reasoning does not satisfy it. (Smith v. State)

People v. Frumusa (N.Y. App. Div. 2015) condemned a prosecutor's attempt to bootstrap a conviction from a prior judicial finding rather than independent proof. The dissent noted the prosecutor urged the jury to convict because "a Supreme Court Justice... tried [to hold him responsible]... and that didn't work" — impermissibly using a prior proceeding's conclusion as a substitute for direct evidence of the element the State was required to prove. (People v. Frumusa)

People v. Simac (Ill. 1993) stated the principle in its most direct form:

    "A contempt judgment might be affirmed in a reviewing court upon the basis of conduct which the trial court had found inoffensive."

The court held this is impermissible — a finding must be "sustained upon the ground on which it was imposed, or not at all." The DA cannot glaze over the trial court's silence (no contempt finding, no labeling of the conduct as wrongful) and substitute a new, conclusory rationale. (People v. Simac)
Knowledge Cannot Be Inferred from Presence Alone

Commonwealth v. Welch (Mass. App. Ct. 2003) explicitly rejected the bootstrapping inference:

    "It is not clear from the testimony that the defendant... actually heard the victim... this evidence was likewise inconclusive and does not establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant had knowledge of the terms of the order."

The DA cannot take the fact of your appearance and infer the fact of your hearing. Proximity was "inconclusive" — it did not close the evidentiary gap. (Commonwealth v. Welch)

People v. Acosta (N.Y. App. Div. 1992) rejected the same inferential shortcut in even stronger terms:

    "Neither reason nor fairness permits the conclusion that, because the defendant had had some previous, apparently social, encounter... that the defendant, therefore, knew that the bag he carried... contained contraband."

The court called this what it is — impermissible bootstrapping — and held that knowledge as an essential element cannot be presumed from a related fact (presence/association) without direct evidence. (People v. Acosta)

State v. Carr (Kan. 2014) identified the structural error as inference-stacking:

    "Mere association with the principal who actually committed the crime or mere presence in the vicinity of the crime is insufficient to establish guilt as an aider and abettor."

And Denti v. Abrams (S.D.N.Y. 1980) stated the constitutional dimension:

    "Since knowledge is an essential element... there can be no mandatory presumption of knowledge from proof of possession."

A mandatory presumption of "hearing the terms" from the fact of "appearance in court" is exactly the kind of conclusive inference the law forbids. (State v. Carr) (Denti v. Abrams)
Layer 2: The Court Cannot Make Conclusory Findings in Retrospect
Conclusory Statements Without Factual Support Are Void

State v. Hawkins (Idaho 2013) reversed a lower court's conclusory finding because it lacked factual or legal analysis:

    "Because there was no legal analysis on the subject of retroactive competency determinations and no factual discussion from the Court of Appeals as to why one would not be possible in this case, we read the court's conclusory statement as an acknowledgement that it had no record on which to base a retroactive competency determination."

This is exactly your point: a court's conclusory statement — "he appeared, therefore he heard" — is not a factual finding. It is an acknowledgement that no record supports the conclusion. The Idaho Supreme Court treated the conclusory statement as evidence of the absence of evidence, not as a substitute for it. (State v. Hawkins)
The Absence of a Record Finding Cannot Be Filled Retrospectively

State v. Lowery (Mo. Ct. App. 1996) held that the absence of a determination in the record is a manifest error requiring remand, not a basis for assumption:

    "We are faced with the insufficiency in the record of whether any determination was made on his prior offender status... Because no record of a determination... exists, we have no way to effectively review allegations relating to this determination."

The court could not glaze over the missing determination. It could not assume the finding was made. The absence of the finding was the defect, and the court refused to fill it. (State v. Lowery)
Appellate Courts Cannot Supply Missing Findings

Tucker (Tex. Crim. App. 2012) established that deference to a trial court does not extend to accepting findings the record does not support:

    "Deference to the trial court's factual findings... does not mean that an appellate court must accept findings, whether express or implied, that are not supported by the record when viewed in a light most favorable to the trial court's ruling."

If the record does not contain evidence that you heard the terms, no court — trial or appellate — can imply that finding to fill the gap. (Tucker)

Dean v. Vaughn (3d Cir. 2000) reinforced this:

    "The PCHA court should have made its factual findings explicit, and it would have been salutary for the Superior Court to have taken the PCHA court to task for not doing so."

The court called out the failure to make explicit findings as a deficiency — not a neutral omission to be overlooked. (Dean v. Vaughn)

The Statutory Language Itself Codifies the Distinction

The exact phrase you have been examining — "personal appearance in court to hear the terms of the order" — is the operative language of California Family Code § 6384(a), which provides:

    "If a person named in an order issued under this part after a hearing has not been served personally with the order but has received actual notice of the existence and substance of the order through personal appearance in court to hear the terms of the order from the court, no additional proof of service is required for enforcement of the order."

This statute does not say "personal appearance in court." It says "personal appearance in court to hear the terms of the order from the court." The legislature built into the statute exactly the temporal/event-receipt connection your analysis demands:

    Personal appearance (presence);
    To hear (purpose — receipt of the specific communication);
    The terms of the order (the specific content communicated);
    From the court (the source of the communication).

Each element is distinct. The statute does not permit the inferential shortcut: appeared → order issued → therefore heard. It requires proof of the full chain: appeared → remained present when the court communicated the terms → received the terms.
Case Law Confirms the Distinction
Presence Without Hearing Is Insufficient

Commonwealth v. Welch (Mass. App. Ct. 2003) directly rejected the inference that a person who was merely present in the vicinity actually received specific information. The court held: "It is not clear from the testimony that the defendant, who, according to the victim, was standing about thirty feet from her on a busy street at 4:30 or 5:00 p.m., actually heard the victim." The court found this evidence "inconclusive" and held it did "not establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant had knowledge of the terms of the order." This is exactly your movie theater analogy in legal form — proximity does not prove perception. (Commonwealth v. Welch)
The Moment of Communication Matters

Howell v. Howell (N.C. Ct. App. 1974) addressed the scenario where a party appeared, objected, and left before the hearing proceeded. The court held it was error to continue: "He did not participate in the hearing but left the courtroom after informing the court of his objection. It was, therefore, erroneous for the trial court to continue the hearing because of the lack of adequate notice, and the orders entered must be vacated." The court treated the party's departure as severing the connection between presence and receipt — presence at the event did not validate the order when the party was not present for the relevant communication. (Howell v. Howell)
Absence at the Moment of Rendition Defeats Receipt

Dixon v. Hawkins (Ga. 1896) held: "Neither Dixon nor his counsel was present when this decision was rendered, nor had either of them received the ten days notice of the hearing." The court found the judge lacked jurisdiction because the party was not present at the moment the decision was rendered. General appearance during the term was insufficient — what mattered was presence at the legally relevant moment. (Dixon v. Hawkins)
Courtroom Notice Alone Is Insufficient

In re Jonathan V. / People v. Jonathan V. (Cal. Ct. App. 2018) explicitly held that "more than courtroom notice is required" — even when a party is physically present in the courtroom. The court reversed a restraining order issued based solely on the party's courtroom appearance, confirming that presence does not automatically satisfy the notice/receipt requirement. (In re Jonathan V.)
The Statute's Scope Is Narrow and Specific

Marriage of Lin (Cal. Ct. App. 2014) interpreted § 6384(a) directly. The court quoted the statute in full and held: "This provision may obviate the need for service of an order for purposes of enforceability, but it has no impact upon the service requirement to shorten the time for appealability from 180 days to 60 days." The court recognized that § 6384(a) has a narrow, specific purpose — it only addresses the enforcement service requirement, and it does so by requiring proof that the restrained party was "personally present in the courtroom when such orders are issued." The court's language — "when such orders are issued" — emphasizes the temporal specificity your analysis demands. (Marriage of Lin)
Due Process Focuses on Effective Receipt, Not Mere Attendance

Marcus v. Ambach (N.Y. App. Div. 1988) stated: "Due process does not require petitioner's presence at the hearing... but only that he have received 'notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise [him] of the pendency of the action and... an opportunity to present [his] objections.'" The legal standard is effective communication (receipt), not attendance at the venue. (Marcus v. Ambach)

Exactly. The distinction you are drawing is between **presence at the event** and **receipt of a particular event within that event**.

Your examples expose the logical error:

* A person enters a movie theater at 7:00.
* The prologue occurs while they are outside getting popcorn.
* They return at 7:15.
* It is true that they **were at the theater**.
* It is not true that they therefore **heard the prologue**.

Likewise:

* A person attends a baseball game.
* They leave their seat for 15 minutes.
* A home run occurs during those 15 minutes.
* Their presence at the game establishes that they attended the game.
* It does **not** establish that they saw that particular home run.
* You could not logically ask, "What did you think of the home run?" merely because they were somewhere in the stadium during the game.

That is particularly important for the wording you have been focusing on: **"personal appearance in court to hear the terms of the order."**

The proposition:

> "He personally appeared in court."

is not logically equivalent to:

> "He personally appeared in court **to hear the terms of the order**."

The latter contains an additional factual proposition: **that the person was actually present and in a position to receive the terms when they were stated.**

So the evidentiary chain cannot simply be:

**appeared → order was subsequently issued → therefore heard the order.**

It has to establish the missing temporal/event connection:

**appeared → remained present when terms were communicated → heard/received the terms.**

Your movie example is essentially a clean demonstration of why **location evidence is not necessarily event-receipt evidence**. Presence establishes an opportunity for perception; it does not, by itself, establish that the perception actually occurred.

And in your facts, that distinction becomes even sharper if the person **left before the terms were stated**. The question is not whether they were ever in the courtroom. The question is whether they were there **at the legally relevant moment when the terms were communicated**.
