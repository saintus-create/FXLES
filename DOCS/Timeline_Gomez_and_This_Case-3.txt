A. COMMISSIONER AUTHORITY — THE MISSING STIPULATION

   THE RULE. Under article VI, section 21 of the California Constitution and
   Code of Civil Procedure section 259(d), a commissioner may act as a
   temporary judge only upon the stipulation of the parties. (Rooney v.
   Vermont Investment Corp. (1973) 10 Cal.3d 351.) A stipulation may be
   implied from conduct — the "tantamount stipulation" doctrine — but only
   where the party knew the officer was a commissioner and participated
   anyway. (In re Horton (1991) 54 Cal.3d 82, 99.) Where a self-represented
   party was never told the officer was a commissioner, no stipulation can be
   implied. (In re Marriage of Djulus (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 1042.)

   THE CONTRARY AUTHORITY. Two DVPA-specific decisions decide the same
   question and should be addressed rather than omitted:
     - Elena S. v. Kroutik (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 570 — a DVPA restraining
       order issued by a commissioner was upheld on implied consent, where the
       respondent did not prove he lacked awareness of the officer's status
       and no local rule required a stipulation on the record.
     - Michaels v. Turk (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 1411 — a DVPA restraining order
       was held VOID where the restrained party's agreement to have a
       commissioner hear the case was not "apparent on the record."
   The distinction that matters: whether the record shows the party was aware.
   That is exactly what the minute orders will show.

   THE APPLICATION. Commissioner Carol Hubner presided at all three hearings
   (03/21/2024, 04/18/2024, 05/16/2024) and issued the Restraining Order
   After Hearing. The record shows no stipulation from either party. Both
   parties were self-represented. Nothing in the record shows either was
   advised of the officer's title or asked to agree.

   THE GAP. The minute orders. They will show whether a stipulation was taken
   on the record, and what was said about the officer's status.

B. MISSING PROOF OF SERVICE OF THE DVRO — THE KNOWLEDGE ELEMENT

   [CORRECTED — THE FIRST DRAFT OVERSTATES THIS] The first draft says
   "without service, the knowledge element is unproven." That is not the law,
   and stating it that way invites a correction. The accurate position:

   THE RULE. Penal Code section 273.6(a) is violated only by an "intentional
   and knowing violation" of a qualifying order. Knowledge is an element the
   People must prove. (CALCRIM No. 2701, element 3.) But proof of service is
   NOT an element — the cases hold that service need not be shown, and that
   the People need prove only that the defendant knew of the order and had the
   opportunity to read it or otherwise become familiar with it. (People v.
   Saffell (1946) 74 Cal.App.2d Supp. 967, 979 [proof of service not
   required]; People v. Poe (1965) 236 Cal.App.2d Supp. 928, 938-941 [must
   have opportunity to read, need not actually read].)

   [CORRECTED — THE CASE NAME] The first draft cites "People v. Faber (2017)
   223 Cal.Rptr.3d 329." The case is reported as People v. Farber (2017) 15
   Cal.App.5th Supp. 41 [223 Cal.Rptr.3d 329] — an appellate division
   decision, persuasive rather than binding. Its actual holding concerns
   sentencing on multiple misdemeanors, and the order at issue was a civil
   harassment order under Code of Civil Procedure section 527.6, not a DVPA
   order. It is weak authority for the knowledge element. Cite CALCRIM 2701
   and Saffell instead.

   THE APPLICATION. The only proof of personal service in the file is dated
   04/23/2024 — before the order of 05/16/2024 existed. No proof of service of
   the order itself appears in the file. The correct characterization: the
   absence of service documentation is a gap in the People's evidence of
   knowledge, and a discovery target — not a legal conclusion that knowledge
   cannot be proved. It is still the strongest evidentiary gap in the case,
   because the People must prove knowledge and the file does not show how they
   would.

   THE CAUTION. The account that deputies came to the residence and that
   Castillo refused the order is double-edged: it could be used to show he was
   told what the order was. It stays out of filed documents, and belongs in
   the defense analysis with counsel.

C. MENTAL HEALTH DIVERSION — CONSENT MUST BE KNOWING AND VOLUNTARY

   THE RULE. Penal Code section 1001.36, subdivisions (c)(2) and (3), require
   that the defendant consent to diversion, waive the right to a speedy trial,
   and agree to comply with treatment. A court's residual discretion to deny
   diversion is "not unfettered" and must be exercised consistently with the
   principles and purpose of the statute; it is limited to situations where
   those purposes would not be achieved, and the statute does not require a
   defendant to display a particular "attitude" or "seriousness." (Vaughn v.
   Superior Court (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 124 [Sept. 17, 2024]; People v.
   Whitmill (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1138, 1155.)

   [CORRECTED — BROWN'S HOLDING] People v. Brown (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 113
   concerns the RETROACTIVITY of the 2023 amendments to section 1001.36, not
   the attitude point. Keep Brown for retroactivity; cite Vaughn and Whitmill
   for the attitude/seriousness proposition.

   THE APPLICATION. The account describes papers put in front of Castillo
   without explanation, two days before a court date, and a court pressing him
   to sign. Consent produced that way is not knowing and voluntary. Diversion
   requires his consent. He has not given it. The order's own findings compound
   this: it recites consent he says he did not give, and refers to him as
   "her" — a finding carried over from another defendant's case.

SECTION 166 / CONTEMPT LINE (relevant if the counts are pleaded under 166):
  Pen. Code section 166 is captioned "Contempt of court"; section 166(c)(1)
    reaches protective-order violations ("shall constitute contempt of court, a
    misdemeanor").
  People v. Greenfield (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 604 — the elements (order made,
    knowledge, ability to comply, disobedience; "willful" = general intent).
  In re McKinney (1968) 70 Cal.2d 8 — such violations are "prosecuted in the
    same manner as other misdemeanors."
  Out-of-state analogues verified: Idaho Criminal Rule 42(b)(1)(A)-(C)
    (judge's personal knowledge from personally observing or hearing);
    In re White, 45 S.W.3d 787 (Tex. App. 2001) (Tex. Fam. Code 157.066);
    Farace v. Superior Court (1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 915.
